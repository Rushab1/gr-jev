# Related work: probability distributions from decision models

Checked 2026-10-11. This is a focused bibliography for the paper in `docs/paper_plan.md`. It covers work that directly tests typed decision models, work on choice-set effects in language models, and the statistical ideas needed to interpret the reported probabilities. An arXiv preprint is identified as a preprint, not as an archival publication.

## The claim that the literature can support

A Choice answer is a probability distribution over the supplied entries. The probabilities sum to one. That mathematical property does not by itself make the probability of an entry an independent probability that the entry is correct, suitable, or needed. The paper tests whether the distribution is stable under changes to order, equivalent entries, and candidate coverage. It also tests calibration when the task has one correct entry. These are separate properties.

For five identical entries, 0.20 per entry is a symmetry reference if the keys carry no information and the task treats the entries as interchangeable. It is not a measured ground-truth probability that each entry is correct. All five entries can be correct at once. The current copies have keys distinguished by spaces, so the manuscript should state that detail and check whether the key representation itself affects allocation.

The wording "the model outputs are not real probabilities" is too broad. The outputs are numbers satisfying the rules of a distribution over the listed entries. The question is **which event each number can validly represent**. TypeSafe's [Choice documentation](https://docs.typesafe.ai/primitives/choice) describes a distribution over options, while its [confidence documentation](https://docs.typesafe.ai/confidence) defines a separate `confidence` field from the highest option probability. The paper's term "confidence" means the highest option probability, so the two quantities must be named separately when TypeSafe documentation is cited. TypeSafe's [Jev 1.13 notes](https://docs.typesafe.ai/model-jaggedness/jev-1.13) already acknowledge some option-order sensitivity.

## Closest studies of typed decision models

| Source | What it tests | Relationship to this paper |
|---|---|---|
| [Hayashi, *Jev Does Not Play Dice*](https://kantahayashiai.github.io/posts/jev-does-not-play-dice/), primary experiment report 2026 | Jev's Choice probabilities for hidden fair draws. On 400 six-sided die trials it selected one face every time with mean top probability 0.829, while the hidden outcome matched 76 times. It also tests Noul and stated forecast probabilities. | Direct precedent for overconfident Choice output when no evidence distinguishes outcomes. The present paper uses labeled, real task lists and compares three decision models across four domains. Cite this before claiming a first probability-semantics result. |
| [Liu, *Does Jev Play Dice?*](https://qiangliu.net/jev-dice/), primary experiment report 2026 | Jev's Choice probabilities under symmetric fair-die prompts, reordered faces, and 720 permutations of a subjective choice. It finds strong first-position effects in the dice tasks, with much smaller order effects on 72 animal descriptions with a single correct answer. | Direct precedent for the interaction between answer ambiguity and order. The paper's duplicate-correct-option tests are a different controlled structure, but the broad finding that Jev's probabilities are unequal under symmetry already exists. |
| [Porcedda, *Jev thinks “I don't know”, but doesn't say it*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.35342), arXiv 2026 | A synthetic dataset with known event probabilities, tested through Jev's Noul, Choice, and Score primitives. It reports a gap between the binary Choice distribution and the constructed event probability. | Direct overlap with numerical meaning of Choice probabilities. It does not test duplicate or multiple valid entries in a list, position effects, or the three decision models in this study. Read this paper before claiming that ours is the first test of Jev's option probabilities. |
| [Li, He, and Li, *Beyond Calibration*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.33209), arXiv 2026 | Jev's answers to logically linked yes/no and three-way Choice questions. It measures complementarity, additivity, and agreement across question forms. | Direct overlap with the Choice-versus-yes/no control. Its labels are mutually exclusive; the present study also tests identical and simultaneously valid entries. It includes an option-order cycle, so order alone is not a novel test. |
| [Lu and Wu, *Benchmarking candidate coverage and rejection policy transfer*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.03387), arXiv 2026 | Laya, Jev, and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct on covered and missing-answer candidate lists, plus transfer of rejection thresholds between tasks. | Direct overlap with missing-answer and NOTA experiments. The distinction here is the joint study of probability allocation, equivalent entries, and order across three hosted decision models and four domains. Do not claim to introduce the missing-candidate problem. |
| [Blankenstein et al., *BiasBusters*](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/biasbusters-uncovering-and-mitigating-tool-selection-bias-in-large-language-models/), ICLR 2026 | Seven LLMs select among functionally equivalent tools. The authors find preferences for particular providers and earlier-listed tools. | The closest published comparison for equivalent tools and positional bias. It studies LLM tool selection and fairness, rather than native probability distributions from decision models. It should be cited prominently, including in the introduction. |
| [Sun et al., *Type-Safe Is Not Error-Free*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.26758), arXiv 2026 | Jev and two open-weight models under swapped option names and definitions. It measures decision flips while the option text is held fixed. | Shows that the identity and name of an entry can affect a typed decision. The present paper tests probability mass over equivalent entries and across list conditions. |
| [Zhang et al., *Same Scores, Different Decisions*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.27678), arXiv 2026 | Jev and nine language models on legal document inference, varying output order and other request conditions. | Close on individual-decision stability and legal data. Its main outcome is correctness under repeated conditions, not the distribution over equivalent entries. |
| [Liu, *Evaluating System One Models for Agent Security Decisions*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.33401), arXiv 2026 | Jev, Laya, Decider, and Nimble on security judgments. It compares accuracy, calibration, and selective automation. | Supports the motivation for testing probability-based action thresholds. Its emphasis is task-specific errors and selective policies. |
| [Li et al., *JEV-as-a-Judge*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.26550), arXiv 2026 | Uses Jev probabilities or confidence to decide when to accept a judgment and when to escalate. | A concrete downstream use of probability thresholds. It does not test list composition or equivalent entries. |
| [Meng, *How far can a commercial decision model's probabilities be trusted?*](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/4t4449jv), preprint 2026 | Jev 1.13.0 on public classification data; reports task- and question-form-dependent top-label calibration. | Direct prior calibration audit. The present study should add distribution-level perturbations and report its calibration metric separately from its symmetry tests. |
| [Li and Chen, *Readout Stability in Prefill-Only Decision Models*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.07716), arXiv 2026 | Seven prefill-only decision-model families under candidate-menu changes. Renormalizing the first distribution predicts later accuracy within 4.2 points, while a probability-level estimator errs by 21.0 points. | Close to the correct-option removal experiment. It specifically warns that stable ranking does not imply stable numerical probabilities. Cite beside any probability-change claim. |
| [Teke, *ufakzeka-karar*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.06744), arXiv 2026 | An open Turkish typed decision model whose candidate-scoring architecture is order invariant. Its sequential comparison head changes 2.3 to 2.8 percent of answers under order changes. | Demonstrates that order invariance can be designed into a decision model. The paper should avoid saying that all decision models must show position bias. |
| [Jevify, primary repository](https://github.com/uspraveen/Jevify), 2026 | Families of logically related questions, including negation and Choice/yes-no comparisons. | A prior community audit of coherence. Its exact figures should be taken from the repository if used in the paper. |

The reported accuracy drop is 30 to 50 percent across models. | The same basic replacement experiment has already been done for LLMs. This paper can compare decision models with that literature, but needs a matched protocol for direct numerical comparison. |

A high top probability on a missing-answer list is relevant only after testing whether a threshold actually separates covered from uncovered cases. |

## Order, equivalent answers, and choice-set effects

| Source | What it tests | Relationship to this paper |
|---|---|---|
| [Zheng et al., *Large Language Models Are Not Robust Multiple Choice Selectors*](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/file/54dd9e0cff6d9214e20d97eb2a3bae49-Paper-Conference.pdf), ICLR 2024 | Option-ID bias and answer changes under permutations in 20 LLMs. | Establishes option-order bias in LLM multiple-choice tests. The present paper measures the native distribution of decision models, including cases where every entry is valid. |
| [Pezeshkpour and Hruschka, *Large Language Models Sensitivity to the Order of Options*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.11483), arXiv 2023 | Multiple-choice accuracy under option permutations. | Further precedent for order sensitivity. Its main outcome is answer accuracy, not equal allocation among identical entries. |
| [Holtzman et al., *Surface Form Competition*](https://aclanthology.org/2021.emnlp-main.564/), EMNLP 2021 | Different strings for the same concept compete for finite language-model probability mass. | Conceptual precedent for probability mass splitting across equivalent answers. The mechanism is string generation, not a native decision-model Choice distribution. |
| [Faro, *The Luce model with replicas*](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jet.2022.105596), *Journal of Economic Theory* 2023 | Formal choice models with duplicate or near-duplicate alternatives, motivated by the red-bus/blue-bus problem. | Supplies the older statistical-choice framing. A probability of selecting an entry may change when a replica is added even when the underlying outcome has not changed. The paper need not claim that the model violates probability theory because it responds to an added entry. |

## Calibration and probability interpretation

| Source | What it tests | Relationship to this paper |
|---|---|---|
| [Guo et al., *On Calibration of Modern Neural Networks*](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v70/guo17a.html), ICML 2017 | Reliability of top-class confidence and temperature scaling. | Standard source for ECE and top-class calibration. ECE on one-correct-answer questions does not test independent suitability of each entry. |
| [Nixon et al., *Measuring Calibration in Deep Learning*](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content_CVPRW_2019/papers/Uncertainty%20and%20Robustness%20in%20Deep%20Visual%20Learning/Nixon_Measuring_Calibration_in_Deep_Learning_CVPRW_2019_paper.pdf), CVPR Workshops 2019 | Differences between top-class, all-probability, and class-conditional calibration metrics. | Supports reporting the exact event and aggregation behind each calibration number. |
| [Jiang et al., *How Can We Know When Language Models Know?*](https://aclanthology.org/2021.tacl-1.57/), TACL 2021 | Whether language-model probabilities in question answering track correctness frequency. | Earlier calibration result for generative models. The present paper tests decision-model distributions and controlled changes to candidate lists. |
| [Kadavath et al., *Language Models (Mostly) Know What They Know*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.05221), arXiv 2022 | Multiple notions of language-model confidence, including the probability that an answer is correct. | Useful to distinguish answer probability, correctness probability, and the probability that the model knows. |
| [Zhao et al., *Calibrating Predictions to Decisions*](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2021/file/bbc92a647199b832ec90d7cf57074e9e-Paper.pdf), NeurIPS 2021 | Defines decision calibration and distinguishes it from top-class and full-distribution calibration. | Gives a formal reason to test whether probabilities support a specified downstream action, rather than treating a low ECE as a guarantee for every use. Its labels are mutually exclusive. |
| [Fisch et al., *Conformal Prediction Sets with Limited False Positives*](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v162/fisch22a/fisch22a.pdf), ICML 2022 | Multi-label prediction sets when a query can have zero, one, or several correct labels. | A formal comparator for the paper's several-correct and no-correct settings. It outputs sets and bounds false positives; it does not claim that one categorical distribution gives independent correctness probabilities for all labels. |

## Dataset papers to cite in methods

[MetaTool](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/hash/bc12914d66b41b6bfc2d3a5decdb498b-Abstract-Conference.html), ICLR 2024; [MMLU](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.03300), ICLR 2021; [HotpotQA](https://aclanthology.org/D18-1259/), EMNLP 2018; [LexGLUE](https://aclanthology.org/2022.acl-long.297/), ACL 2022. These describe the original datasets. The paper must separately document how its own candidate lists, duplicate entries, NOTA entries, and labels were constructed.

## Positioning for the manuscript

1. Cite Hayashi, Liu, Porcedda, and Li et al. in the first related-work paragraph. All four already question the meaning of Jev probabilities.
2. Cite BiasBusters and TypeSafe's Jev 1.13 notes beside the order and equivalent-entry experiments. Both already report related effects. Cite Teke as a contrasting architecture.
3. Cite Tam et al. and Lu and Wu beside the absent-answer experiments. Those experiments support a comparison and a broader test battery, but are not individually new.
4. Cite Li and Chen beside the candidate-removal experiment. Compare observed probabilities with renormalizing the original distribution; a change caused only by normalization is expected for a Choice distribution.
5. State the proposed contribution as a **controlled characterization of the entire Choice distribution** under order, equivalent entries, multiple valid entries, and missing entries, across Jev, Liquid d1, and GPT-6 Luna Decisions on tool, knowledge, passage, and legal tasks. Verify the final results before putting numerical claims in the abstract.
6. Keep top-option probability, TypeSafe's computed `confidence`, correctness calibration, and per-entry applicability separate throughout the paper.
