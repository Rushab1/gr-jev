\begin{abstract}
%% abstract
A decision model receives a query and a list of options and returns one probability per option. We test whether that probability can be read as the probability that the option is correct. We run Jev, Liquid d1 and GPT-6 Luna Decisions on MetaTool, MMLU, HotpotQA and ECtHR. The lists have one correct option, several correct options, no correct option, or five copies of the same correct option. When a list has two to four correct options, the one with the highest probability has a mean probability of 0.66 to 0.85. The one with the lowest probability has a mean probability of 0.11 to 0.19. TypeSafe's documentation uses a probability threshold of 0.25 in its example code. That threshold keeps every correct option in 15\% to 35\% of these answers. Given five identical copies of a correct option, Jev gives its highest copy a probability of 0.29 to 0.34 and its lowest a probability of 0.10 to 0.12. The two other models give their highest copy a probability of 0.50 to 0.81. When no option is correct, Jev and Liquid d1 still answer every list, with a mean top probability of 0.49 to 0.87. The mean top probability is within 0.14 of the accuracy only when exactly one option is correct. A decision-model probability describes how the model divides one list. It does not reliably say whether an option is correct.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
%% introduction
Decision models do not write text. A decision model gets a query, an instruction and a list of options. It returns one probability for each option. The probabilities in one answer sum to 1. Jev, from TypeSafe AI, has this interface. Liquid d1~\citep{liquid2026introducing} and GPT-6 Luna Decisions~\citep{openai2026decisions} accept the same requests.

The vendors present these numbers as probabilities that a program can act on. TypeSafe's documentation says that ``outcomes assigned a probability of \texttt{0.2} should occur about 20\% of the time''~\citep{typesafe2026primer}. Its page on the Choice question shows a support ticket about a wrong shoe size and a double charge. The answer gives the returns team a probability of 0.61 and the billing team a probability of 0.35. The page says ``the ticket belongs to two teams'', and its example code notifies a second team when that team's probability is above 0.25~\citep{typesafe2026choice}.

That use treats each number as the probability that one option is correct. We test that reading. We build lists with exactly one correct option, several correct options, no correct option, or five copies of the same correct option. We then compare the probabilities with what the reading predicts.

Table~\ref{tab:example} shows the problem on one ticket. Jev gives each of its two problems' teams a probability of 1.00 when that problem is sent alone. When the two problems are sent together, Jev gives the teams probabilities of 0.68 and 0.32. Both teams are still correct. Their probabilities now add up to 1.

Our runs on three models and four datasets give four results.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Several correct options.} One correct option gets a mean probability of 0.66 to 0.85. The lowest correct option gets 0.11 to 0.19, close to the highest distractor. A probability threshold of 0.25 keeps every correct option in 15\% to 35\% of the answers.
\item \textbf{Identical options.} Five copies of one correct option do not get equal probabilities. Jev gives the copy with the highest probability about three times as much as the copy with the lowest probability. The other two models give one copy a probability of 0.50 to 0.81.
\item \textbf{No correct option.} Jev and Liquid d1 answer every such list, with a mean top probability of 0.49 to 0.87.
\item \textbf{One correct option.} The mean top probability is within 0.14 of the accuracy. Here the documented reading works.
\end{itemize}
A removal test explains the first result. When we remove the correct option with the highest probability, the second correct option's probability rises from between 0.11 and 0.19 to between 0.53 and 0.71. The probability of an option is its share of the list. It is not a judgement of that option alone.

\section{Setup}
%% setup
\paragraph{The Choice question.} Each request has a state, which is our query. It also has a question of type \texttt{choice}, with an instruction and a list of named options. Each option has a description. The answer has the selected option, one probability per option, and a field named \texttt{confidence}. We call the selected option the prediction. We call the highest probability in the answer its confidence. This is not the \texttt{confidence} field, which TypeSafe computes from the highest probability and the number of options~\citep{typesafe2026confidence}.

\paragraph{Models.} We use Jev version \texttt{jev-1.13.0} through TypeSafe's API. We use Liquid d1 (\texttt{liquid/d1}) and GPT-6 Luna Decisions (\texttt{openai/gpt-6-luna-decisions}) through Vercel AI Gateway~\citep{vercel2026models}. The gateway accepts the same requests and does not take a model version. All runs were made on 11 October 2026. The three models got the same requests, and each request was sent once. Jev and GPT-6 Luna Decisions return probabilities with two decimals. Liquid d1 returns full floating-point values. GPT-6 Luna Decisions can return an answer of type \texttt{refusal}, with no probabilities. Jev and Liquid d1 each received 202,088 questions and refused none.

\paragraph{Datasets.} We use four datasets whose labels say which options are correct. MetaTool~\citep{huang2024metatool} has a query and a list of tools, with one or two correct tools. MMLU~\citep{hendrycks2021mmlu} has a question and four choices, one of them correct. We use a seeded sample of 1,000 questions. HotpotQA in the distractor setting~\citep{yang2018hotpotqa} has a question and up to ten passages, two of which support the answer. We use its 7,405 validation questions. ECtHR Task~A of LexGLUE~\citep{chalkidis2022lexglue} has the facts of a court case and ten articles of the European Convention on Human Rights. Of its 1,000 test cases, 655 have one violated article, 192 have two to four violated articles, and 153 have none. We also wrote 24 support tickets after TypeSafe's example (Section~\ref{sec:tickets}).

\paragraph{Lists.} We send four kinds of lists. (1)~\emph{Several correct options}: the example's list as released, for the MetaTool queries with two correct tools, the HotpotQA questions, and the ECtHR cases with two to four violated articles. (2)~\emph{Identical options}: a list with five copies of one correct option, sent in its five cyclic permutations. (3)~\emph{No correct option}: five random options from other examples, or the example's own list without its correct options. (4)~\emph{One correct option}: the example's list. When several options are correct, the instruction asks for all of them, for example ``Choose every passage in the list that supports the answer to the question.'' Appendix~\ref{app:lists} gives every instruction.

\paragraph{Measures.} Accuracy is the share of answers whose prediction is a correct option. For lists with several correct options, we report mean probability separately for the highest correct option, the lowest correct option and the highest distractor. We also report the share of answers in which every correct option has a probability above 0.25, the threshold in TypeSafe's example code. All figures are means over the lists that a model answered.

Table~\ref{tab:allocation} shows that the models give most of the probability to one correct option. The highest correct option gets a mean probability of 0.66 to 0.85. The lowest correct option gets a mean probability of 0.11 to 0.19. The highest distractor gets a mean probability of 0.04 to 0.17. The two ranges overlap. For GPT-6 Luna Decisions on MetaTool, the highest distractor's probability (0.17) is above the lowest correct tool's probability (0.11).

The models still rank the options well. The prediction is a correct option in 0.82 to 0.98 of the answers. The problem is the number, not the ranking. A program that keeps every option with a probability above 0.25 keeps all the correct options in 15\% to 35\% of the answers. It misses a correct option in the other 65\% to 85\%.

The instruction does not change this. On MetaTool, Jev gives the lowest correct tool a probability of 0.14 when the instruction says ``Choose one of them.'' and 0.15 when it says ``Choose both.'' On HotpotQA, the instructions ``may support'', ``supports'' and ``definitely supports'' give the lowest supporting passage probabilities of 0.18, 0.17 and 0.17.

\paragraph{The documented example.}\label{sec:tickets} We sent the ticket from TypeSafe's Choice page once. Jev returns a probability of 0.65 for returns, 0.31 for billing and 0.04 for shipping. The page shows probabilities of 0.61, 0.35 and 0.04. So the documented answer holds for this ticket. We then wrote 24 tickets the same way. We used 12 pairs of problems. Each ticket describes two problems, and each problem belongs to a different team. We sent each pair twice, once in each order. Table~\ref{tab:tickets} gives the results. The second team's probability is above 0.25 in 11 of the 24 tickets on Jev, 12 tickets on Liquid d1 and 3 tickets on GPT-6 Luna Decisions. When we send each of the 24 problems alone, its team gets a probability of 0.99 to 1.00. The models know which teams apply: the third team gets a probability of 0.00 to 0.01. They do not give the two teams that apply a number that says so.

\paragraph{Removing the highest correct option.} We sent each list again without its highest correct option. Before removal, the second correct option's probability is 0.11 to 0.19. After removal, it is 0.53 to 0.71 (Table~\ref{tab:removal} in the appendix). This is close to what sharing out the removed probability in proportion predicts. Liquid d1 returns full-precision probabilities. The prediction gives probabilities of 0.64, 0.80 and 0.70 on MetaTool, HotpotQA and ECtHR. The model gives probabilities of 0.61, 0.70 and 0.67. The models follow Luce's choice axiom~\citep{luce1959individual} approximately. The second correct option was low because the first one held the probability, not because the model judged it wrong.

In a list of five copies of one correct option, no option is better than the others. If the probability showed whether an option is correct, the five copies would get the same probability. Table~\ref{tab:copies} shows that no model does this. Jev gives its highest copy a mean probability of 0.29 to 0.34 and its lowest copy 0.10 to 0.12, about a third of that. Liquid d1 gives the highest copy a mean probability of 0.50 to 0.81 and the lowest copy 0.03 to 0.08. GPT-6 Luna Decisions gives the highest copy a mean probability of 0.67 to 0.79 and the lowest copy 0.01 to 0.02.

The position decides which copy is highest. Liquid d1 and GPT-6 Luna Decisions predict the first copy in 86\% to 100\% of their answers. Jev predicts the last copy in 74\% of its answers on MMLU and 59\% on ECtHR.

When a list has no correct option, every option's probability of being correct should be low. Table~\ref{tab:abstention} shows that Jev and Liquid d1 answer every such list, with a mean confidence of 0.49 to 0.87. On lists with one correct option, Jev's mean confidence is 0.86 to 0.94, and Liquid d1's is 0.81 to 0.89. A confidence threshold does not separate the two kinds of lists.

GPT-6 Luna Decisions behaves differently. It refuses 58\% to 98\% of the lists of random options and 1\% to 27\% of the lists made of the example's own options. On the lists that it answers, its mean confidence is 0.65 to 0.87. The API gives no reason for a refusal (Appendix~\ref{app:refusals}).

The models can say that no option is correct when the list lets them. When we add a ``none of the above'' option to four random options, the models predict it in 0.93 to 1.00 of the answers.

\subsection{One correct option}
%% calibration
With exactly one correct option, mean confidence minus accuracy is $+0.08$, $+0.09$ and $+0.14$ on MetaTool for Jev, Liquid d1 and GPT-6 Luna Decisions. It is $+0.02$, $+0.05$ and $+0.04$ on MMLU, and $-0.03$, $-0.01$ and $0.00$ on ECtHR (Table~\ref{tab:calibration} in the appendix). Calibration is usually measured with exactly one correct option~\citep{guo2017calibration}. This is the one case where the documented reading holds. With several correct options, mean confidence is 0.08 to 0.23 below accuracy.

\section{Related work}
%% related
\paragraph{Tests of Jev's probabilities.} \citet{deusser2026evaluating} find that Jev's Choice probabilities are well calibrated on 37 datasets with one correct label. Our lists with one correct option agree with this. Other studies find failures outside that case. \citet{shankaranarayana2026beyond} report that Jev gives one option up to 0.80 when the input has no information about the answer. \citet{zhong2026when} report that Jev rejects 7\% of arithmetic lists that lack the correct answer, even with an explicit rejection option. \citet{lu2026benchmarking} find that rejection thresholds do not carry over from one task to another. \citet{porcedda2026jev} and \citet{li2026beyond} ask the same question in several forms and find that Jev's probabilities do not fit together as probabilities should. \citet{li2026readout} study open models built after Jev. They restrict the first answer to a changed list and renormalise it. This predicts the new accuracy within 4.2 points, but a probability-level version of the estimate is off by 21.0 points. Our removal test compares mean probabilities on three hosted models and finds agreement within 0.13.

\paragraph{Option lists in language models.} The order of the options changes the answers of language models to multiple-choice questions~\citep{zheng2024large,pezeshkpour2024large}, and models asked to choose among equivalent tools fix on one provider or prefer the tools listed earlier~\citep{blankenstein2026biasbusters}. Accuracy falls when ``none of the above'' is the correct answer~\citep{tam2025none}. When several answers are correct, the best of 27 models selects exactly the correct set in 41.8\% of questions~\citep{xu2025sata}.

\paragraph{This paper.} We measure the whole Choice distribution of three decision models in one design, on lists whose number of correct options is known: one, several, none, and five copies of the same one. We compare the result with the threshold that the vendor's documentation uses.

\section{Limitations}
%% limitations
Each request was sent once, and Jev does not always give the same answer to the same request. The figures have no margins of error yet. We tested three models on one day, and the gateway gives no version for two of the models.

The labels have limits. A HotpotQA label marks a passage that contains a supporting sentence. When asked a yes/no question about one supporting passage alone, the models give it mean probabilities of 0.45, 0.40 and 0.52. So some labelled passages may not be correct options on their own. The ECtHR labels are the Court's findings. The tickets avoid this problem. When sent alone, each problem gets its team with a probability of 0.99 to 1.00. There are only 24 tickets. Claude Opus 5.5 wrote them, and no second reader checked them.

The copies in a list of identical options have names that differ by trailing spaces. The API needs different names. Jev and GPT-6 Luna Decisions return two decimals, so the renormalised values in the removal test are exact only for Liquid d1. The MMLU runs use 1,000 questions.

\section{Conclusion}
%% conclusion
A decision model divides a total probability of 1 among the options in a list. That share ranks the options well. It says whether an option is correct only when exactly one option in the list is correct. When the list has several correct options, identical options, or no correct option, the probability does not mean what the documentation says it means. A program should not apply a fixed threshold to the probability of a second option. If it needs to know whether each option applies, it can ask about each option separately.

\section{Terms and measures}
\label{app:terms}
An \emph{option} is one entry of the list sent with a query: a tool on MetaTool, a choice on MMLU, a passage on HotpotQA, an article on ECtHR, a team for a ticket. A \emph{correct option} is an option that the dataset labels as correct for the query. A \emph{distractor} is an option that is not correct. The \emph{prediction} is the option in the \texttt{choice} field of the answer. The \emph{confidence} of an answer is its highest probability. \emph{Accuracy} is the share of answers whose prediction is a correct option. A \emph{refused list} is a list for which the model returns an answer of type \texttt{refusal} and no probabilities.

\section{Refusals of GPT-6 Luna Decisions}
\label{app:refusals}
GPT-6 Luna Decisions refused 24,286 of its 202,088 questions (12.0\%). It refused 18,639 of 26,460 lists of random options, 3,458 of 17,231 lists made of the example's distractors, 1,545 of 39,530 lists that have only correct options, 526 of the 103,912 other lists, and 118 of 14,955 yes/no questions. A refusal has no probabilities and no reason. We sent one refused request again seven times, with one change each time. The model answered the same passages when the query was changed to one that a passage fits. It answered the same query when a ``none of the above'' option or a fitting passage was added to the list.

\section{Support tickets}
\label{app:tickets}
TypeSafe's example has three teams: returns (``Exchanges, wrong or damaged items''), shipping (``Delivery status, delays, lost packages'') and billing (``Charges, invoices, payment problems''). We kept these teams, their descriptions and the instruction ``Which team should handle this?''. Claude Opus 5.5 wrote 24 problems, eight for each team, on 11 October 2026. We joined them into 12 pairs of problems for two different teams and sent each pair in both orders. The file \texttt{src/grjev/stimuli/tickets.json} in the repository has every problem and pair.
