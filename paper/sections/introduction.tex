Decision models are emerging as an alternative to generative language models for applications requiring structured decisions, offering lower inference costs and latency by returning predictions directly rather than generating text \citep{almeida2026jev,liquidAI2026d1}. Three recently introduced models are TypeSafe AI's Jev \citep{almeida2026jev}, Liquid AI's d1 \citep{liquidAI2026d1}, and OpenAI's GPT-6 Luna Decisions \citep{openai2026decisions}. Given contextual information, instructions, and a set of candidate choices, all three models return a probability distribution over the choices. Providers emphasize the reliability and practical utility of these output probabilities. TypeSafe states that ``Higher probability should correspond to a greater chance that the answer is correct'' \citep{typesafe2026primer}, while Liquid AI describes its decision models as returning ``calibrated probabilities across a fixed set of outcomes'' \citep{liquid2026models}. OpenAI recommends setting probability thresholds for downstream routing and filtering \citep{openai2026decisions}. TypeSafe further demonstrates using such thresholds to select additional correct choices beyond the highest-ranked choice \citep{typesafe2026choice}.


%The providers present these outputs as probabilities rather than ranking scores. Each value therefore needs an interpretation. Applying a threshold to select correct choices treats each value as the probability that its choice is correct. These claims and recommendations raise a question for tasks with multiple correct choices. Can normalized model probabilities reliably identify all correct choices, rather than only the highest-ranked one?

When several choices are correct, the events that each choice is correct are not mutually exclusive. Their probabilities need not sum to 1. Yet the providers recommend thresholds on these outputs (Table~\ref{tab:typesafe}). For probability thresholding to reliably identify multiple correct choices, correct choices should receive higher probabilities than distractors. Further, equally relevant choices should receive equal shares. The constraint allows this. Two equally relevant choices in a list of 10 can each receive 0.5, and five identical copies can each receive 0.2. We study the allocation of unit probability over choices passed to the model.

Consider the HotpotQA question ``Who is older, Maria Sharapova or Chanda Rubin?'' \citep{yang2018hotpotqa}. The question is accompanied by 10 candidate passages. One of those passages gives Rubin's date of birth and another gives Sharapova's. Both passages together are sufficient, but neither is sufficient alone. However, all three decision models assign a probability higher than 0.90 to one passage and a probability lower than 0.10 to the other as detailed in Table~\ref{tab:example}. The behavior persists even after anonymization. Appendix~\ref{app:example} lists the full set of original and anonymized passages. In this paper, we measure how decision models distribute probability when a single correct choice does not exist. We look at scenarios where the choices are ambiguous or multiple choices are correct.



We use three diverse  datasets which have lists with more than one correct choice - MetaTool~\citep{huang2024metatool} for tool selection, HotpotQA~\citep{yang2018hotpotqa} for supporting passages and ECtHR~\citep{chalkidis2022lexglue} for legal classification. When multiple choices are correct, we examine the gap between the maximum and minimum probabilities assigned to correct choices. On all datasets, probability allocation is asymmetric and concentrated on the model's top choice. The mean gap ranges from 55 to 72 percentage points. Thresholding does not reliably recover all correct choices. We then modify the datasets' choice lists to present five identical copies of a single correct choice. Identical choices should receive identical probability. However, we observe that the mean gap between the maximum and minimum probabilities ranges from 17 to 79 percentage points. When each choice is asked as a separate yes/no question, we observe miscalibration on intermediate probabilities.

% Our results show that decision-model probabilities are not reliable for identifying multiple correct choices. When a list contains more than one correct choice, the mean gap between the highest and the lowest probabilities assigned to correct choices ranges from 55 to 72 percentage points across the three models and three datasets. A uniform distribution over \(n\) choices assigns \(1/n\) to each, representing no preference among them. The models rank every correct choice above every distractor in 59\% to 85\% of the lists. In 28\% to 46\% of those lists, a correct choice receives less than \(1/n\). Further, even when the models rank every correct choice above every distractor, the best single threshold gives the exact set in only 62\% to 77\% of those lists, and that threshold is low, ranging between 0.02 and 0.07, against the 0.25 that TypeSafe uses as an example in its documentation (Section~\ref{sec:results}).

When multiple choices are correct, normalized model probabilities cannot be interpreted as independent probabilities of correctness. On yes/no questions about each choice, the ECE is 3 to 9 percentage points over all bins and 8 to 18 percentage points without the bin from 0 to 0.1.

We then test allocation when choices are mutually exclusive and ambiguous. We use ChaosNLI \citep{nie2020chaosnli} in the next experiment, where we compare the allocation of model probabilities with human votes on mutually exclusive labels. Even when annotators disagree, model probabilities are concentrated on the model's top choice. Models exhibit overconfidence relative to the 100 human annotators. When the vote shares of the two labels with the most votes differ by less than 10 percentage points, the models' probabilities on them differ by 66 percentage points on average.
