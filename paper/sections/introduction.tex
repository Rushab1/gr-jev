Decision models are emerging as an alternative to generative language models for applications requiring structured decisions, offering substantially lower inference costs and latency by returning predictions directly rather than generating text \citep{almeida2026jev,liquidAI2026d1}. Three recently introduced models are TypeSafe AI's Jev \citep{almeida2026jev}, Liquid AI's d1 \citep{liquidAI2026d1}, and OpenAI's GPT-6 Luna Decisions \citep{openai2026decisions}. Despite differences in their implementations, these models expose a similar choice-based interface - given contextual information, instructions, and a set of candidate choices, they return a probability distribution over the choices, with probabilities summing to one. Their providers emphasize the reliability and practical utility of these outputs. TypeSafe states that "Higher probability should correspond to a greater chance that the answer is correct" \citep{typesafe2026primer}, while Liquid AI describes its decision models as returning "calibrated probabilities across a fixed set of outcomes" \citep{liquid2026models}. OpenAI recommends setting probability thresholds for downstream routing, filtering, and review \citep{openai2026decisions}, and TypeSafe demonstrates using such thresholds to select additional alternatives beyond the highest-ranked choice \citep{typesafe2026choice}. These claims and recommendations motivate an important question: how do decision-model probabilities behave when more than one choice is correct?


Consider a HotpotQA question \citep{yang2018hotpotqa}: ``Who is older, Maria Sharapova or Chanda Rubin?'' The question comes with a list of 10 passages. To answer the question using only the supplied passages, both birth dates are needed. One passage gives Rubin's date and the other gives Sharapova's, so the two passages together are sufficient, but neither is sufficient alone. However, all three decision models assign a probability higher than 0.90 to Rubin's passage and a probability lower than 0.10 to Sharapova's passage. Choice probabilities must sum to one, so the 10 passages share the probability mass. To obtain a separate probability for each passage, we ask each model a yes/no question about each one: ``Does this passage support the answer to the question?'' The probabilities of ``yes'' on Jev, Liquid d1, and GPT-6 Luna Decisions are 0.31, 0.16 and 0.47 for Rubin's passage and less than 0.15 for Sharapova's passage. Table~\ref{tab:example} shows the question, the two relevant passages and the probabilities. After replacing the names, tournaments, dates and locations with invented ones, the Choice probabilities remain uneven. Jev and Liquid d1 still favor the older player's passage, but GPT-6 Luna Decisions now favors the younger player's passage. Appendix~\ref{app:example} lists the full set of original and anonymized passages, along with the probabilities assigned by each model.


Recent work on decision models has measured accuracy and calibration on datasets with one correct choice. \citet{deusser2026evaluating} evaluate Jev on 37 datasets and report that its Choice probabilities are calibrated on their tasks. They also find that rotating options leaves aggregate accuracy unchanged. \citet{li2026beyond} compare Jev's answers to logically related yes/no and Choice questions and find differences between probabilities for events that should agree. TypeSafe states that the order of choice options can affect Jev's answer \citep{typesafe2026jev}. \citet{li2026readout} find that restricting and renormalizing the original probabilities predicts aggregate accuracy after options are removed. For generative language models, \citet{zheng2024large,pezeshkpour2024large} show that option order changes answers to multiple-choice questions. \citet{blankenstein2026biasbusters} find that language models selecting among functionally equivalent tools can favour particular providers. \citet{tam2025none} find that replacing the correct MMLU answer with ``None of the above'' lowers LLM accuracy. \citet{wang2025leastincorrect} find that LLMs can select an offered answer even when none is correct. However, many modern AI tasks require selecting more than one correct choice. Examples include selecting the tools an agent needs to complete a task, the diagnoses supported by a medical record, the passages relevant to a query in retrieval-augmented generation, and the vulnerability types present in code.


In this paper, we present a systematic evaluation of the reliability of decision-model probabilities for identifying multiple valid alternatives. We specifically examine the gap between the maximum and minimum probabilities assigned to correct choices. We start with evaluating the models on the choices lists in the datasets. We then modify the datasets' choice lists while keeping the query and the instruction fixed. First, we present five identical copies of a correct choice with the expectation that same choices must produce equal probabilities. Second, we compare the gap across alternative formulation that asks one yes/no question per choice. We focus on three decision models: TypeSafe AI's Jev, Liquid AI's d1, and OpenAI's GPT-6 Luna Decisions and study their behavior on four datasets: MetaTool \citep{huang2024metatool}, MMLU \citep{hendrycks2021mmlu}, ECtHR \citep{chalkidis2022lexglue}, and HotpotQA \citep{yang2018hotpotqa}.

Our results show that decision-model probabilities are not reliable for identifying multiple valid alternatives through thresholding. When a list contains more than one correct choices, the mean gap between the highest and the lowest probability ranges from 55 to 74 percentage points across the three models and four datasets. A threshold of 0.25, which TypeSafe receommends, identifies all correct choices in only 15\% to 35\% of the answers. The probabilities are uneven even when all choices are identical and assigned probabilities to identical choices range from 0.17 to 0.78.  We conclude that the model assigned probability is therefore not a measure of how strongly that choice is supported.

When a list contains one correct choice among distractors, Jev's mean confidence differs from its accuracy by at most 9 percentage points, consistent with \citet{deusser2026evaluating}.


\begin{itemize}
\item Decision models do not write text. Input: a query, an instruction, a list of choices. Output: one probability per choice, summing to 1. Jev (TypeSafe AI), Liquid d1~\citep{liquid2026introducing} and GPT-6 Luna Decisions~\citep{openai2026decisions} share this interface.
\item What the vendors say about the probabilities. TypeSafe's primer: ``outcomes assigned a probability of \texttt{0.2} should occur about 20\% of the time''~\citep{typesafe2026primer}. Liquid AI: decision models ``return calibrated probabilities''~\citep{liquid2026decision}. OpenAI's guide advises thresholds set on labelled examples~\citep{openai2026decisions}.
\item The use that we study: a threshold on the probabilities, to find every valid choice. TypeSafe's Choice page: a ticket about a wrong shoe size and a double charge gets 0.61 (returns) and 0.35 (billing); the page says ``the ticket belongs to two teams''; its example code notifies a second team above 0.25~\citep{typesafe2026choice}.
\item The problem: a threshold finds every valid choice only when each valid choice gets a high probability. The probabilities of a list sum to 1. Two valid choices cannot both be close to 1.
\item Design: the choice lists of four datasets as released, with one correct choice or with two to four. Separate experiments: five identical copies of one correct choice, and permutations of the order of a list.
\item Result 1, several correct choices: the gap between the highest and the lowest correct choice is 55 to 74 percentage points. The highest gets 0.66 to 0.85, the lowest 0.11 to 0.19, close to the highest distractor at 0.04 to 0.17. A 0.25 threshold keeps every correct choice in 15\% to 35\% of the answers.
\item Result 2, identical choices: the gap between the highest and the lowest copy is 17 to 78 percentage points. Equal probabilities are 0.20 each. The position decides which copy is highest: Liquid d1 and GPT-6 Luna Decisions select the first copy in 86\% to 100\% of their answers, and Jev selects the last copy in 74\% of its answers on MMLU and 59\% on ECtHR.
\item Not in the abstract, the explanation: when the highest correct choice is removed, the second one rises from between 0.11 and 0.19 to between 0.53 and 0.71. The low probability comes from the list, not from the choice.
\item Not in the abstract, one correct choice: mean confidence is within 0.14 of the accuracy. The vendors' statements hold here. Moving the correct answer from the first to the last position changes the accuracy by 13 points at most, and by 2 points or less on Jev.
\item Not in the abstract, no correct choice: Jev and Liquid d1 answer every list, with a mean confidence of 0.49 to 0.87. GPT-6 Luna Decisions refuses 1\% to 98\% of these lists.

\item Not in the abstract, ``None of the above'': with the correct choice of an MMLU question replaced by it, on 12,981 questions, Jev, Liquid d1 and GPT-6 Luna Decisions select it in 62\%, 71\% and 57\% of their answers. The 28 LLMs of \citet{tam2025none} average 35\%. The accuracy falls by 30, 15 and 28 points, against 39 on average for the LLMs. \citet{tam2025none} keep only the questions that they label as suited to ``None of the above'', and do not publish the labels.
\item Not in the abstract, a way around the shared list: one yes/no question per choice finds 80\% to 96\% of the violated ECtHR articles and 38\% to 51\% of the HotpotQA supporting passages.
\item Wording of the claim: the probabilities are not reliable for finding several correct choices with a threshold. We do not say that they are wrong in general.
\end{itemize}
