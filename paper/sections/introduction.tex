Decision models are emerging as an alternative to generative language models for applications requiring structured decisions, offering lower inference costs and latency by returning predictions directly rather than generating text \citep{almeida2026jev,liquidAI2026d1}. Three recently introduced models are TypeSafe AI's Jev \citep{almeida2026jev}, Liquid AI's d1 \citep{liquidAI2026d1}, and OpenAI's GPT-6 Luna Decisions \citep{openai2026decisions}. Despite differences in their implementations, these models expose a similar choice-based interface. Given contextual information, instructions, and a set of candidate choices, they return a probability distribution over the choices, with probabilities summing to one. Their providers emphasize the reliability and practical utility of these outputs. TypeSafe states that "Higher probability should correspond to a greater chance that the answer is correct" \citep{typesafe2026primer}, while Liquid AI describes its decision models as returning "calibrated probabilities across a fixed set of outcomes" \citep{liquid2026models}. OpenAI recommends setting probability thresholds for downstream routing, filtering, and review \citep{openai2026decisions}, and TypeSafe demonstrates using such thresholds to select additional correct choices beyond the highest-ranked choice \citep{typesafe2026choice}. These claims and recommendations raise a question for tasks with multiple correct choices. Can normalized Choice probabilities reliably identify all correct choices, rather than only the highest-ranked one?

While categorical distributions must output probabilities that sum to 1, providers document that these are calibrated and recommend thresholds in practice. Since probabilities are a share of the total of 1, with more correct choices, the share of each correct choice goes down on average. However, since thresholding is the recommended way to use these models, for multiple correct choices, each correct choice must have a baseline probability. The constraint still allows an even split. Two correct choices in a list of 10 can each receive 0.5, and five identical copies can each receive 0.2. We hence study the allocation of unit probability over choices passed to the model.

Consider the HotpotQA question ``Who is older, Maria Sharapova or Chanda Rubin?'' \citep{yang2018hotpotqa}. The question is accompanied by 10 candidate passages. One of those passages gives Rubin's date of birth and another gives Sharapova's. Both passages together are sufficient, but neither is sufficient alone. However, all three decision models assign a probability higher than 0.90 to Rubin's passage and a probability lower than 0.10 to Sharapova's passage. The Choice probabilities therefore concentrate on one of the two passages needed to answer the question. Table~\ref{tab:example} shows the question, the two relevant passages and the probabilities. After replacing the names, tournaments, dates and locations with invented ones, the Choice probabilities remain uneven. Jev and Liquid d1 still favor the older player's passage, but GPT-6 Luna Decisions now favors the younger player's passage. Appendix~\ref{app:example} lists the full set of original and anonymized passages, along with the probabilities assigned by each model.


Recent work on decision models has measured accuracy and calibration on datasets with one correct choice~\citep{porcedda2026jev}. \citet{deusser2026evaluating} evaluate Jev on 37 datasets and report that its Choice probabilities are calibrated on their tasks. They also find that rotating choices leaves aggregate accuracy unchanged. On yes/no questions, Jev's probabilities rank well but are poorly placed relative to a threshold of 0.5. \citet{liu2026dice} finds that Jev gives unequal probabilities to the six faces of a fair die. Both studies test Jev only. \citet{li2026beyond} compare Jev's answers to logically related yes/no and Choice questions and find differences between probabilities for events that should agree. TypeSafe states that the order of choices can affect Jev's answer \citep{typesafe2026jev}. \citet{li2026readout} find that restricting and renormalizing the original probabilities predicts aggregate accuracy after choices are removed. For generative language models, \citet{zheng2024large,pezeshkpour2024large} show that choice order changes answers to multiple-choice questions. \citet{blankenstein2026biasbusters} find that language models selecting among functionally equivalent tools can favour particular providers. \citet{wang2025leastincorrect} find that LLMs can select an offered answer even when none is correct. However, modern AI tasks require selecting more than one correct choice. Examples include selecting the tools an agent needs to complete a task, the diagnoses supported by a medical record, the passages relevant to a query in retrieval-augmented generation, and the vulnerability types present in code.


In this paper, we measure how decision models distribute probability over several correct choices. We specifically examine the gap between the maximum and minimum probabilities assigned to correct choices. We first evaluate the models on the choice lists in the datasets. We then modify the datasets' choice lists while keeping the query and the instruction fixed to present five identical copies of a correct choice. Identical copies differ only in their place in the list and their random IDs, so they should get the same probability. We also check calibration on yes/no questions and observe miscalibration on intermediate probabilities. We study their behavior on three datasets, MetaTool \citep{huang2024metatool}, ECtHR \citep{chalkidis2022lexglue}, and HotpotQA \citep{yang2018hotpotqa}.

Our results show that decision-model probabilities are not reliable for identifying multiple correct choices. When a list contains more than one correct choice, the mean gap between the highest and the lowest probabilities assigned to correct choices ranges from 55 to 74 percentage points across the three models and three datasets. A uniform distribution over \(n\) choices assigns \(1/n\) to each, representing no preference among them. The models rank every correct choice above every distractor in 52\% to 85\% of the lists. In 28\% to 50\% of those lists, a correct choice receives less than \(1/n\). Further, even when the models rank every correct choice above every distractor, the best single threshold gives the exact set in only 64\% to 78\% of those examples, and that threshold is low, ranging between 0.02 and 0.08, against the 0.25 that TypeSafe uses as an example in its documentation (Section~\ref{sec:results}).

The allocation of probabilities is uneven even when a list contains five identical copies of a correct choice. The mean gap between the highest and lowest (identical) copies ranges from 17 to 79 percentage points.
For lists containing exactly one correct choice, Jev's ECE is 8 percentage points on MetaTool and 3 on ECtHR, consistent with \citet{deusser2026evaluating}. When multiple choices are correct, normalized Choice probabilities cannot be interpreted as independent probabilities of correctness. On yes/no questions about each choice, the ECE is 3 to 9 percentage points over all bins and 8 to 18 percentage points without the bin from 0 to 0.1.
