Decision models return a probability distribution over supplied choices conditioned on the context supplied in the query.
These probabilities are often interpreted as the chance that each choice is correct.
Many standard benchmarks test whether models can pick one or more correct choices, but do not evaluate the probabilities attached to those outcomes.
We test this claim on Jev, Liquid d1 and GPT-6 Luna Decisions with choice lists built from four diverse datasets covering tool selection   (MetaTool), multiple-choice question answering (MMLU), supporting-snippet selection for question answering (HotpotQA) and legal judgement prediction (ECtHR). From the benchamrks' own choice lists, we create new choice lists by premutating the choices, removing the correct choice(s), presenting the same correct choice multiple times and removing the top-ranked correct choice.
We study how the probabilities of decision models are affected by the number of correct choices in the list and by the order or the chocies. When multiple correct choices exist we find that models concentrated probabilities on a single correct choice - the spread of probabilities between the highest and lowest ranked correct choice was between 55 to 74 percentage points. Further, when five identical choices were presented, the model's  probabilities were far from being uniformly distributed, with the difference between the highest and lowest ranked identical choice ranging from 17 to 78 percentage points. We further noticed that models tend to concentrate probabilities on the first or last positions, and for Jev, it is dependent on the dataset. We conclude that decision model probabilities are correctly concentrated when a single option is correct. However, when multiple options are correct, the output probabilities of decision models do not reliably represent the probability of each option being correct, and models are often overconfident in their predictions.

On a separate note, decision models are often more comfortable picking None as the correct choice compared to LLMs - compare our evaluation on the MMLU dataset with Tam et al's reported results on the same dataset. We also find that decision models are more robust to the presence of adversarial choices in the list, and are less likely to be fooled by them.
